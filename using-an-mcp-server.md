Problem 1 (25%) · AI and task costs
A marine survey firm needs a fixed bundle of chart annotation, image sorting, and report formatting. Labor costs \(w=240\) kroner per hour. AI costs \(r=90\) kroner per processing hour. Both methods produce the same quality, and there are no setup costs.
Task	Human hours per unit	AI hours per unit	Share of human-only cost
Chart annotation	0.5	2	20%
Image sorting	0.75	1	30%
Report formatting	1	2	50%


(a) Derive the automation cutoff from unit costs. State how you handle a tie, then identify the cheaper method for each task.

(b) Derive and calculate the percentage cost saving for the bundle using the baseline cost shares as fixed weights.

(c) Repeat the calculations when \(r\) falls to 60 kroner per hour. In at most three sentences, explain how the set of automated tasks and the cost-saving index behave at a cutoff.
Problem 2 (30%) · Comparative advantage
Alder and Bracken produce kayaks (\(x\)) and sails (\(y\)) using labor alone. Both have competitive markets, full employment, and identical preferences \(U=x^{1/2}y^{1/2}\). Use sails as the numeraire.
Country	Labor hours available	Hours per kayak	Hours per sail
Alder	120	2	3
Bracken	180	6	4


(a) Derive the autarky relative prices from the equal-wage condition. Calculate the prices and identify each country’s comparative advantage.

(b) Assume complete specialization. Use world market clearing to derive and calculate the free-trade relative price. Verify that it supports the assumed specialization. Calculate both wages in units of sails.

(c) Calculate Alder’s consumption bundle. Draw its production frontier and trade budget line, with \(x\) horizontal and \(y\) vertical. Label both frontier intercepts and the production and consumption points. In at most three sentences, explain how the figure shows a gain from trade.
Problem 3 (30%) · Heckscher–Ohlin and factor prices
Fenland makes rope (\(x\)) and metal fittings (\(y\)). Each unit of rope requires 3 labor hours and 1 machine hour. Each unit of fittings requires 1 labor hour and 2 machine hours. Fenland has \(E=110\) labor hours and \(K=70\) machine hours. Markets are competitive, and both factors are fully employed. World prices are \(p_x=14\) and \(p_y=13\).

(a) Write the zero-profit and full-employment equations in general notation. Identify the labor-intensive good and calculate factor rewards and outputs.

(b) The world price of rope rises to 16. The price of fittings and both endowments stay fixed. Calculate the new factor rewards. Use Stolper–Samuelson to explain how each factor reward changes in units of both goods.

(c) Return to the original prices. Labor rises to \(E=125\), and \(K\) stays fixed. Calculate the new outputs and explain the result using the Rybczynski theorem in at most three sentences.
Problem 4 (15%) · Monopolistic competition and gravity
Two economies with identical technologies and factor endowments open trade. In each country, bicycle helmet firms sell differentiated models, pay a fixed production cost, and can enter freely. Consumers value variety.

(a) In at most four sentences, explain why both countries can import and export helmets. Connect fixed costs, market power, and differentiated varieties.

(b) In at most three sentences, explain how free entry can yield zero economic profit even when price exceeds marginal cost. Describe one gain consumers can get from trade.

(c) Which statement follows from the standard gravity model, holding other determinants fixed? Choose one.
- A. Greater distance between trading partners tends to increase bilateral trade.
- B. Larger economies tend to trade more with each other.
- C. Two economies with the same GDP must trade equally with every partner.
- D. Distance affects trade only when the partners produce different kinds of goods.
